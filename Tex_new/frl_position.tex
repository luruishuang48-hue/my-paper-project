\title{Position, Not Capability:\\How Stock Markets Price Large Language Model Releases}

\author[sdu]{Zhuo Chen\corref{cor1}}
\ead{zhuochen@sdu.edu.cn}
\author[sdu]{Ruishuang Lu}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\affiliation[sdu]{organization={Center for Economic Research, Shandong University},
city={Jinan}, country={China}}

\begin{abstract}
Do stock markets price the capability of large language models, or the
economic position of firms around a release? Using 124 day-verified model
releases (2022--2026), benchmark capability scores, and double-blind-coded
ecosystem positions for Nasdaq-100 firms, we find capability is not
positively priced: the slope is zero pooled, significantly negative among
closed-source flagships, and zero among unrelated firms. Position is priced:
hardware suppliers and rival developers earn 1.6--1.7 percentage points over
twenty days, with immediate abnormal volume, while firms deploying AI in
conventional businesses show precisely zero price and volume response.
Capability matters only as an amplifier of the hardware premium.
\end{abstract}

%% ============================================================
\section{Introduction}
%% ============================================================

Large language model (LLM) releases have become routine, salient information
events---124 benchmark-qualified releases in our 2022--2026 sample. A natural
reading, implicit in exposure-based studies of AI and asset prices
\citep{eisfeldt2023,babina2024}, is that markets reward technological
capability: the more capable the model, the larger the revaluation of exposed
firms. This letter tests that reading directly and rejects it. Markets price
\emph{where a firm sits} in the AI production chain, not \emph{how capable}
the released model is.

We combine three measurement inputs that are, to our knowledge, new in
combination. First, release dates verified to the day against first-party
sources (vendor blogs, release notes, archived first captures) for every
event. Second, a direct measure of model capability---Artificial Analysis
benchmark scores---rather than a proxy. Third, ecosystem positions
(hardware supplier, cloud platform, integrator, deployer, competitor,
investor, owner) assigned to each (firm, developer) pair by two independent
coders under a fixed codebook (agreement 99.0\%, Cohen's $\kappa$
0.84--1.00). The firm universe is the Nasdaq-100, so selection is exogenous
to AI exposure and the thirty-one constituents with no AI relationship
provide a within-event control group.

Three results follow. First, capability is not positively priced. The pooled
slope of twenty-day cumulative abnormal returns (CARs) on capability is zero;
among closed-source releases---where capability rents would be most
appropriable \citep{teece1986}---it is significantly \emph{negative}
($-0.8$\pp{} per standard deviation of capability, $p=0.02$); among firms
with no ecosystem relationship it is an exact zero, as a placebo requires.
The negative flagship slope is an anticipation gradient: the most capable
releases are the most rumored, so their information reaches prices before the
event window. Second, position is priced. Hardware suppliers earn
1.66\pp{} and rival model developers 1.61\pp{} over $[0,+20]$
($p\approx0.01$), with abnormal trading volume on the announcement day
itself; deployers---firms using AI inside conventional businesses---show a
CAR of $-0.07$\pp{} (SE 0.25\pp{}) and no abnormal volume: a genuine zero,
not offsetting disagreement. Third, the two dimensions interact: capability
carries no average price but amplifies the hardware premium (interaction
$p=0.088$), exactly as derived-demand logic implies---a more capable model is
bigger news for compute suppliers, not for the average ``exposed'' firm.

The letter's contribution is to unbundle ``AI exposure.'' Exposure rankings
conflate firms on opposite sides of the same shock; once positions are
separated, the pricing loads on the compute-complementarity channel and on
industry information spillovers to rivals \citep{lang1992}, while the
displacement channel that dominates public commentary is priced at zero. A
companion full-length paper documents when this pricing rule formed
(2025, with the reasoning paradigm) and shows that two opposite headline
results in an earlier hand-collected vintage of the data were artifacts of
compound measurement error; here we hold the reproducible pipeline fixed and
focus on the cross-sectional question.

%% ============================================================
\section{Data}
%% ============================================================

Events intersect the AI Timeline chronology with Artificial Analysis (AA)
benchmark coverage, which screens out releases too minor to be benchmarked;
identity matches and all manual decisions are recorded in auditable decision
tables. Of 134 candidate events, 125 have day-verified dates and 124 enter
the main sample (3 events from 2022, 8 from 2023, 45 from 2024, 59 from
2025, 9 from 2026); 87 are language-model releases with AA intelligence
scores, 47 are open-weight. Capability is the AA intelligence index of the
event's flagship model (SD 12.7 in the estimation sample).

For each of the 124 events and 101 Nasdaq-100 firms we compute market-model
CARs (S\&P~500 proxy, estimation window $[-200,-10]$, minimum 120
observations) over $[0,+w]$, $w\le20$, and standardized abnormal log volume;
three-factor CARs serve as robustness. Firm controls (log assets,
book-to-market with a negative-equity flag, momentum, volatility) are matched
under a strict 45-day as-of rule. Positions at the (firm, developer) level
are aggregated to the event level by union over the event's developers.
Standard errors are clustered by event (CR2) throughout; specifications with
event fixed effects identify from within-event variation only.

\section{Results}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Capability is not priced}

Table~\ref{tab:capability} regresses CAR$[0,+20]$ on the capability score
with firm controls and year fixed effects. The pooled slope is $-0.0001$ per
index point ($p=0.51$). Among the 50 closed-source releases the slope is
$-0.0006$ ($p=0.024$; $-1.3$\pp{} per SD under the three-factor benchmark,
$p<0.001$); among the 37 open-weight releases it is 0.0000 ($p=0.93$). Among
unrelated firms the slope is zero ($p=0.80$)---capability scores do not
predict returns where no economic channel exists, so the closed-source
negative is not a mechanical artifact. Replacing the level with a surprise
measure (the score's distance above the pre-event frontier maximum) yields
the same pattern, because benchmark surprise still correlates with flagship
status: the releases that move benchmarks most are the ones markets see
coming. Column (5) shows the interaction that survives: capability
amplifies the hardware premium ($+0.0010$ per index point, $p=0.088$; the
hardware main effect at mean capability is $+2.07$\pp{}, $p=0.010$).

\subsection{Position is priced}

Table~\ref{tab:position} regresses the same outcome on the seven position
indicators. Hardware suppliers earn 1.66\pp{} over unrelated constituents
($p=0.012$; 1.79\pp{} under three factors; 1.54\pp{} with event fixed
effects), and rival developers earn 1.61\pp{}---a positive competitor
spillover indicating that releases convey industry-level growth information
that dominates competitive displacement \citep{lang1992,chen2005}. The
deployer coefficient is $-0.07$\pp{} with a 95\% confidence interval of
$[-0.56,+0.42]$\pp{}: the market does not price LLM releases as bad news for
firms that merely use AI. The premium is not a single-stock artifact---
dropping any one hardware firm leaves it between 1.2 and 1.6\pp{}, and
excluding NVIDIA gives 1.5\pp{} ($p=0.023$).

\subsection{Volume separates attention from irrelevance}

Abnormal volume completes the taxonomy (Table~\ref{tab:volume}). Hardware
suppliers trade 0.12 standard deviations above normal on the announcement
day ($p<0.001$)---attention is immediate even where same-day price response
is nil, and directional consensus forms over the following weeks. Deployers
show no abnormal volume at any horizon ($p=0.74$ at $[0,+1]$): their zero
price response reflects irrelevance, not offsetting disagreement. Cloud
platforms are the third fate: the largest abnormal volume in the panel
(0.29 SD at $[0,+1]$, and already 0.20 SD in the pre-event window) with no
directional price response---the signature of investors interpreting the
same signal with opposite priors \citep{kandel1995}, natural for firms for
which a frontier release is simultaneously compute-rental good news and
own-model bad news.

\subsection{Robustness}

The position premia are significant in 16--17 of 20 specifications spanning
benchmarks (S\&P~500, Nasdaq-100, SOX, three-factor), windows, winsorization,
date-confidence subsamples, multi-component-event exclusion, and event fixed
effects; the deployer zero and a null hardware~$\times$~open-weight
interaction ($p=0.90$) hold in all twenty. Announcement timestamps recovered
for 55 events show only two after-hours releases; shifting all events by one
trading day leaves the results unchanged. The capability results are
insensitive to using capability surprise, to three-factor benchmarks, and to
restricting to firms with any AI relationship.

%% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%% ============================================================

Around LLM releases, equity markets do not pay for capability---they pay for
position. Benchmark scores carry no positive premium and a negative one among
anticipated flagships; hardware suppliers and rival developers revalue by
1.6--1.7\pp{} while AI-deploying firms show precisely zero price and volume
response; and capability matters only by scaling the hardware premium. For
research that ranks firms by AI exposure, the lesson is that exposure
conflates positions with opposite pricing; for the public debate, four years
of releases contain no evidence that markets price AI displacement on
announcement.
